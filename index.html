What is a pro se litigant?

Someone who represents themselves, without a lawyer. Pro se is Latin for “on one’s own behalf.”

You have the right to do it. It is written into the law precisely so that justice does not depend on being able to afford it. That is a real right, and it is worth something.

But what if you have never been inside a courtroom? What if you do not know where to start? That is what this is for.

In Montana, you cannot take a discrimination claim straight to court.

The law makes you start with the Montana Human Rights Bureau. You file your complaint there first, within 180 days of the last discriminatory act, and the Bureau’s process has to run its course before the case can go to district court.

Skip that step, or miss that deadline, and you can lose the claim entirely. Everything in this tool is built around that process.

Montana Human Rights Act, Mont. Code Ann. Title 49, chapter 2, part 5; the 180-day deadline is § 49-2-501.

Why I want to teach this

Institutions count on you walking away. They know most people who are discriminated against or harassed cannot afford a lawyer, and that most will give up before they start. That is not a side effect. It is the advantage.

Pro se litigation has the power to take one person’s plight and turn it into real protection for others. A finding closes a gap. A policy gets rewritten. A procedure that failed one person gets fixed before it reaches the next.

And it puts an institution on notice. An organization that has been found to have discriminated, or that agreed in writing to change something, cannot credibly claim not to have known the next time. A record exists.

My case will end, one way or another. I do not want it to end for nothing.

This is its legacy: helping others find accountability through pro se litigation.

What a case file is, and what it is for

A case file is the organized record of what happened to you — every date, every document, every person who saw it, kept in one place and in an order someone else can follow.

It is not the complaint. The complaint is two pages. The case file is everything behind it.

So you can answer any question in ten seconds. When an investigator asks when something happened, or whether you have proof of it, the answer is in front of you instead of somewhere in your memory.

So the facts survive. Posts get deleted. Memories blur. Records go missing. What you captured and filed at the time is what remains.

So patterns become visible. One incident is a feud. Nine incidents in date order, with a document behind each, is a case.

And so you can hand it to someone. An attorney, an investigator, a mediator. A pile of screenshots is not evidence. An organized record is.

Before anything else: what are you actually claiming?

Not everything unfair is unlawful. The Bureau can only act where the treatment was because of a protected ground — sex, race, color, national origin, religion, age, disability, marital status, pregnancy, or because you complained about discrimination — in a covered area such as employment, housing, education, public accommodations, or government services.

And this is the part that catches people

The Bureau investigates what your complaint says, and nothing else. Not what the facts imply, not what is obvious from reading it.

If you were worn down over a year, you may think the complaint plainly describes a hostile work environment. It does not, unless you write those words. If you resigned because it became intolerable, that is constructive discharge — a separate claim. If something happened after you complained, that is retaliation — separate again.

Three things happened. Name one and only one gets investigated.

This is where I got stuck, and it is the first thing the Complaint page walks you through.

The intake worker does this every day and you do not. Describe everything in order and let them tell you what each thing is called — then read their draft before you sign it.

Start documenting now — from the guide

Screenshot everything, immediately

Posts get deleted. Comments get edited. A trustee in my case published a post admitting that my training stopped after I complained, then edited that sentence out four days after my cease-and-desist. I had screenshotted it the day it went up, so I have both versions — which means I can show not only what he admitted, but exactly what he went back and removed.

The edit is its own evidence. A person who quietly deletes one sentence four days after a legal notice has told you which sentence mattered.

Not everything can be saved that way. A woman who threatened me deleted a post carrying over 111 comments after she called the police on me, and those comments are gone. The difference between the two outcomes was timing.

Capture it the day you see it. Not the week after.

How to screenshot so it counts

Include the whole frame. Name, date, timestamp, reaction count, comment count. A cropped quote proves nothing.

Capture the thread, not just the comment. Who they were replying to matters.

Get the reaction list. Tap into who reacted. In my case, an Assistant Chief reacting with a heart to a public post attacking me is a very important exhibit — it establishes institutional ratification, because a supervisor endorsed the conduct rather than merely failing to stop it. It only exists because I opened the reaction list.

Screenshot your own phone’s date and time showing. It is on the status bar. Do not crop it off.

Capture the profile too. A screenshot of someone’s page showing their name and relationship to the respondent ties the comment to the case.

Metadata — the part nobody thinks about

Every photo and screenshot carries hidden data: the date and time it was created, and sometimes where, and what device made it.

This can prove things nothing else can. In my case the metadata on an image someone posted publicly recorded that it had been saved from Messenger before it was posted — meaning the image was circulated privately first. That one detail turned several unconnected posts into evidence of coordination.

Working with metadata

Look at it. On an iPhone, open the photo and swipe up, or tap the info button. On Android, open the photo and tap details. You will see the date, time, device, and sometimes the source app.

Screenshot the metadata panel itself. That screenshot is your exhibit.

Do not edit, crop, or re-save the original. Every time you do, you risk stripping it. Keep the untouched original and work from copies.

Beware of sharing methods that strip it. Texting a photo or uploading to some platforms removes metadata. Transfer originals by AirDrop, cable, or direct upload to cloud storage.

Your own photos carry it too. A photo I took of a note left in the station kitchen on my first training night carried a timestamp proving when it happened. I did not plan that. It was simply there.

Check their website. Then check it again.

Public bodies publish an enormous amount without realizing what is in it. Agendas, packets, minutes, audio and video recordings, budgets, policy manuals, personnel rosters, board appointments. All of it sitting there, free, needing no request at all.

I found some of my most important evidence on a city council website. A recording of a public meeting — days after I first spoke publicly — in which a firefighter’s spouse stood up during public comment and said something about me that was not true. That recording was posted by the City itself. Nobody had to hand it to me.

What to look for

Meeting recordings and minutes. Compare the two. Minutes are a summary written by a person. A recording is what was actually said. The gap between them is often the evidence.

Agendas and packets. These contain the documents given to decision-makers before a vote — letters, reports, recommendations. Often more revealing than the minutes.

Budgets and pay schedules. What a position is funded at, and who is actually being paid.

Policy manuals and handbooks. Note the adoption dates. A policy adopted shortly after you complained is itself a fact.

Board rosters and appointment records. Who sits on what, and since when.

Download it the day you find it

Websites change. Pages get edited. Recordings go missing. Documents get quietly replaced with different versions.

Save the file itself, not a link. Screenshot the page showing the file listed, with the date visible. Note the URL and the date you captured it.

Check the Wayback Machine at web.archive.org for how the page looked before. If something has been altered or removed, a prior capture may still exist — and the difference between the two is its own evidence.

Gaps matter too. If a body posts recordings for every meeting and then stops for a stretch, that absence is a documented fact you can ask about in writing.

And it is free and anonymous. Nobody is notified that you looked. You can do this tonight, before you have decided anything.

Contemporaneous records

A journal written the day it happened is worth more than any reconstruction. Date at the top. What happened, who was present, what was said, what you did about it. Factual. No adjectives you would not say under oath.

Text yourself. Email yourself. Tell one friend in writing. All of those are timestamped by a third party and are far harder to challenge than your memory.

In my case, texts I sent my partner in real time during an incident became evidence that the incident happened as I described it. I was not building a case. I was just telling someone.

§ 45-8-213(2)(a)(iii) excepts anyone given warning of the recording — and adds that if one person gives the warning, either party may record.

So say it out loud at the start: “Before we go on, I am recording this call.” If they stay on the line, you are inside the exception. If they object, stop.

Say it on the recording itself, not in a message beforehand. The announcement and their response are then part of the file, which is exactly where you want them.

Public meetings — § 45-8-213(2)(a)(ii) excepts anyone speaking at a public meeting.

The statute also reaches only a hidden device, and a public place carries no reasonable expectation of privacy. Hold the phone where it can be seen.

But not behind a closed door — a private meeting with your supervisor is a private conversation. The official-duty exception in (2)(a)(i) protects the official who records, not you. Announce it.

Organizing it — from the guide

A folder of three thousand screenshots is not evidence. It is a pile. The difference between the two is structure, and structure is what lets you find the one document that answers a question in the ten minutes you have before a call.

How I organize

There is no single right system. This is mine, and it survived two years and several thousand files.

By respondent — One folder per entity you are complaining about. Everything touching that entity goes in its folder, even if it also belongs somewhere else. Duplicate rather than agonize.

By person — For individuals who appear repeatedly. I have folders for specific people whose conduct spans years. When a new post appears, it goes straight in.

By event — For things that happened on one date and generated many documents — a meeting, an incident, a campaign.

By source — Records produced to you, separated by who produced them and when. Label with the date of production. This matters when they produce something late, or produce different versions.

Filings — What you have sent and what they have sent. Every version. Never overwrite.

Working — Drafts, notes, half-finished analysis. Keep it separate from evidence so you never confuse the two.

The other exceptions

§ 45-8-213(2)(a)(ii) also excepts anyone speaking at a public meeting outright.

Where the line actually is

A public meeting, a counter, a lobby, an officer in the street — recording openly is on solid ground. Hold the phone where it can be seen.

A witness is not only someone who saw the incident. It is anyone who can speak to a fact you need established — who was in the room, what the practice was before you arrived, what they were told afterwards, or that a document exists.

Keep this list private. Do not publish it, and think carefully before naming people to the respondent before you have to. Someone who is willing to help you may become unwilling once their employer knows.

Note who you have actually spoken to. A name on a list is not a witness until they have said what they are prepared to confirm.

How to ask someone to be a witness

Ask once, plainly, and let them decide. Pressure produces a reluctant witness, and a reluctant witness is worse than none at all.

Tell them exactly what you are asking. Not “will you back me up” but “you were in the room on 9 June — would you be willing to tell an investigator what you heard?” Specific is easier to say yes to.

Tell them what it involves. Usually a phone call from an investigator lasting under an hour. They are not going to court. Most people picture a witness box and say no to that.

Do not coach them. Never suggest what they should say, never show them your complaint first, never tell them what another witness said. If it ever comes out that you did, their account is worth nothing and yours is damaged with it.

Ask what they are comfortable confirming. Someone who will confirm two facts firmly is more valuable than someone who vaguely agrees with everything.

Accept a no gracefully and record it. People have mortgages. A no today may become a yes later, and burning the relationship guarantees it never does.

Do not give the investigator a name you have not spoken to

A witness who is surprised by the call will usually say the minimum, and the minimum tends to be “I don’t really remember.” That lands in the file as a witness who did not support you.

Written statements — when and why

A written statement is usually worth more than a verbal account, for one reason: it is fixed. Memory drifts, people soften under pressure from an employer, and a phone interview is summarized by someone else in their own words. A statement written at the time, in the witness’s own words, does not change.

What a useful statement contains

Who they are and their relationship to the people involved — job title, how long, how they know you.

Only what they personally saw or heard. First-hand. If they are reporting something someone else told them, it must say so plainly, with who said it and when.

Dates, or their best honest estimate. “On or about the second week of June” is fine and is better than a precise date they are not sure of.

The test

A good statement is short, specific and boring. Three paragraphs of what someone saw beats two pages of how strongly they feel about it.

Not everyone is your ally

This is the part nobody warns you about, so I will.

Some people will attach themselves to your case for their own reasons. Because they dislike the same person. Because it is interesting. Because being close to it gives them something. Their interest in your case is not the same as loyalty to you, and it can evaporate the moment it costs them anything.

And some will say one thing to you and another to an investigator. People who are certain in a kitchen become careful on a recorded call with their employer’s name in the first sentence. That is not always malice. Sometimes it is simply fear, and the effect on your case is identical.

What this looks like in practice

A complainant lists several witnesses. The investigator interviews one. That one gives an account that contradicts her. The case closes with no reasonable cause. Shortly afterward, that witness is promoted.

Whether the promotion was connected is not something anyone can prove. But the case was lost on a single interview with a single person who had a reason to want the complaint to fail.

So protect yourself

Do not tell people more than they need to know. Not your strategy, not your other witnesses, not what is in your file. Anything you say may be repeated to the respondent, sometimes within the hour.

Think about who benefits if you lose. Anyone whose job, standing or relationships improve if your complaint fails should be assumed to be a hostile witness, however friendly they are to your face.

Note it in this list. If you have real doubt about someone, say so in their notes rather than putting them forward and hoping.

A witness you are unsure of is a risk you are choosing to take. One bad interview can sink a true complaint. It is not disloyal to leave someone off a list.

Why evidence matters more than witnesses

The cases that win are the cases with documents behind them. That is the plain truth of it, and it is not entirely fair, because plenty of real discrimination happens with no paper at all.

A document does not get nervous. It does not need its employer’s permission. It does not change its account between the kitchen and the phone call. Build your case so that it survives every single witness letting you down.

If you have no evidence, file anyway. You cannot know what a records request will produce until you send one, and you certainly cannot know what the respondent will put in writing once they have to answer. Plenty of evidence arrives after you file, from them.

Evidence people forget they have

Your own phone. Photos with timestamps and locations. Texts. Call logs showing who called whom and when. Screen time and location history that place you somewhere on a date.

Calendars and schedules. Shift rosters, sign-in sheets, training logs, meeting invitations, room bookings. These establish who was present without anyone having to remember.

Minutes, agendas and recordings. Public bodies publish them. What is missing from a set of minutes is evidence too.

Websites, as they were. The Internet Archive holds old versions of pages. A policy, roster or statement that has since been quietly changed may still exist there.

Metadata. When a photo was taken, when a document was created and last edited, when a file was saved. Often more useful than the content.

Their own public statements. Social media posts, newsletters, press quotes, job adverts. People say things publicly that contradict what they later tell an investigator.

Payroll and HR records. Pay stubs, performance reviews, disciplinary records, your personnel file, who was promoted and when. You are entitled to much of this.

Comparators. What happened to someone else in your position. Public salary data, published rosters, promotion announcements.

Your own contemporaneous notes. A journal entry written on the day carries real weight, even though you wrote it, because it was written before you knew how this would end.

What you sent and what they did not answer. An unanswered email, dated, is evidence of what you raised and when — and of their response to it.

Almost everything I was able to prove came from records someone else created and had to hand over. Not from my account of events. From their own documents, contradicting their own statements.

Who is subject to a request

In Montana the right to know is in the Constitution itself — Article II, Section 9 — not merely in statute. It reaches public bodies and agencies of state and local government.

That includes: cities and towns, counties, school districts, fire districts and other special districts, state agencies and departments, public universities, and the boards and committees of all of them.

It does not reach private employers. If your employer is private, this route is closed — but your own personnel file may still be available under a different rule, and discovery in litigation reaches further than a records request ever will.

What people miss

Text messages on personal devices about public business are public records. So are emails sent from a private account about government work. The test is the content, not the device.

Meeting recordings are records. So are draft minutes, calendars, call logs, and the metadata on documents.

Ask for the search terms they used. If a production comes back thin, asking what they searched and which accounts they searched is often more revealing than the production itself.

How to make the request

In writing, always — email is fine. Keep the sent copy. The date you asked is as important as what you asked for.

Be specific about scope — name the date range, the people whose records you want, and the accounts — official and personal — you believe hold responsive material.

Ask for categories, not conclusions — “all communications between X and Y between these dates” works. “Anything showing they discriminated” does not.

Ask about fees up front — Montana allows a charge for the actual cost of fulfilling a request. Ask for an estimate before they begin, and ask whether a waiver is available.

Follow up in writing when it is late — and keep following up. A paper trail of unanswered requests is itself evidence.

Ask again, differently — I obtained the two documents most central to my case only after a different body produced them from an account the first search had excluded.

If they are a public body, the meeting rules are yours too

Montana’s open meeting law is not just about watching. It gives you things you can use, and it is written to be liberally construed in favor of openness.

They cannot act on an item that is not on the published agenda — § 2-3-103. A decision taken on something the public was never told would be discussed is vulnerable — and a district court can void a decision made in violation of the open meeting law.

You have a right to comment on matters not on the agenda — the agenda must include an item allowing public comment on any public matter within the agency’s jurisdiction that is not otherwise listed. They do not get to keep your subject off the agenda and thereby keep you quiet.

What you say must go into the minutes — § 2-3-103 requires public comment received at a meeting to be incorporated into the official minutes. If you speak, there is a permanent record of it — and if the minutes omit or distort what you said, that omission is itself documented.

Minutes must be kept and available — for every meeting required to be open. Compare them against the recording.

When they can close a meeting — and when they cannot

Individual privacy. § 2-3-203(3). But the right belongs to the person being discussed — and if that person waives it, the meeting must be open. If they are closing a session to talk about you, you can waive and force it open.

Litigation strategy. § 2-3-203(4). Only where an open meeting would have a detrimental effect on their litigating position — and never where the only parties to the litigation are public agencies.

The possibility of a closed session must appear on the agenda so the public is notified in advance.

And the decision itself must be made in open session. They may discuss strategy behind a closed door. They may not decide behind one.

Minutes of a properly closed session are sealed absent a court order — but minutes of that closed portion must still be kept. The absence of them is a fact.

What becomes public afterward

A settlement paid by a public body is generally a public record. Public money is public. So are the minutes approving it, and in many cases the agreement itself.

This cuts both ways and you should know it before you sign anything. A confidentiality term cannot un-public a public expenditure, and a term that purports to may not be enforceable against the record itself. Ask explicitly what the body will disclose if someone requests it.

It also means you can look up what they paid to settle with other people. Prior settlements, verdicts, and the budget lines they came out of are often discoverable before you ever walk into mediation.

Your own records

Separately from any public records request, ask for your own file. Personnel file, training records, disciplinary records, your application, anything with your name on it.

Ask in writing. Ask for the complete file, including anything held by a supervisor outside the official file. Ask for it in native format with metadata intact, not printed and rescanned.

Why your own file matters

My personnel file arrived 39 days late and contained a written warning I had never seen or been told about. It was dated, signed, and filed without my knowledge.

It also showed what was not there — no record of the training that had been halted, no record of a commitment that had been made to me in writing elsewhere. Absences in a file are evidence too.

Ask early. If you wait until you are in a dispute, a litigation hold may be invoked to delay it.

Step one: have you been legally harmed, and under which law?

Not everything unfair is unlawful. Being treated badly, singled out, or pushed out is not by itself something the Bureau can act on. The law reaches a narrower thing, and it is worth being honest with yourself about whether you are inside it before you write anything.

The three questions

1. Is there a protected ground? Was the treatment because of your sex, race, color, national origin, religion or creed, age, disability, marital status, pregnancy, or because you complained about discrimination? If none of those is in it, the Bureau has no jurisdiction, however badly you were treated.

2. Is it in a covered area? Employment, housing, education, public accommodations, credit and insurance, or the services of a state or local government body.

3. Was the treatment because of that ground? This is the part that carries the case. Not that you are a woman and were treated badly — that you were treated badly because you are a woman. A comparator is usually how this gets shown: someone outside your group, in a comparable situation, treated better.

If you cannot answer all three, file anyway and ask

Call the Bureau and describe what happened. That conversation is free and they will tell you whether it is something they can take. A great many people talk themselves out of a valid complaint because they assumed the answer.

Name every claim, explicitly

This is where I got stuck, and where most people do.

The Bureau investigates what the complaint says, and only that. It does not investigate what the facts suggest, what you clearly meant, or what is obvious from reading it. If a claim is not named, it is not in the case.

What that means in practice

If you were worn down over a year by comments, exclusion and hostility, you may think the complaint obviously describes a hostile work environment. It does not — unless you write those words.

If you resigned because conditions became intolerable, that is constructive discharge, and it is a different claim from the discrimination that caused it. Name it separately.

If something happened after you complained, that is retaliation, which is its own violation with its own 180 days. Name it separately again.

Three things happened to you. If you only name one, only one gets investigated.

Claims to consider naming

Disparate treatment — you were treated worse than someone comparable, because of a protected ground.

Hostile work environment — conduct severe or pervasive enough to alter the conditions of your work. Say the words.

Retaliation — something adverse happened because you complained, testified, or opposed discrimination.

Constructive discharge — you resigned because the conditions were intolerable.

Failure to accommodate — a disability or religious accommodation was refused or ignored.

Disparate impact — a rule or test that applies to everyone but screens out one group at a much higher rate, and has never been validated.

Denial of services or benefits — for claims against a government body.

Use the intake interview for exactly this

The intake worker does this every day and you do not. Describe everything that happened, in order, and let them tell you what it is called. That is the single most useful thing they do.

Then read the draft they write before you sign it, line by line, against your own account. If a claim you described is missing from the draft, say so before you sign. It is far easier to get it in now than to amend later.

You can amend a complaint, and amendments can relate back to the original filing date. But that is a thing you have to ask for and argue. Getting it right first is better.

The words — from the guide

You cannot argue with a system whose language you do not speak. Learn these first.

The two sides

Charging party — what the Bureau calls the person filing the complaint. You will also see complainant. Same person.

Respondent — the business or entity the complaint is against. Montana’s Act reaches organizations, not individuals personally.

The three layers

There are three possible stages of review, and knowing which one you are in tells you what can happen next.

Human Rights Bureau — the neutral state agency that receives complaints and conducts the informal investigation. The Bureau does not award damages.

Office of Administrative Hearings — where a case goes after a reasonable cause finding. A hearing examiner holds a contested case hearing and can award damages. Older material calls this the Hearings Bureau.

Human Rights Commission — a five-member citizen panel appointed by the Governor. Hears appeals from either a Bureau dismissal or a hearing examiner’s decision.

Words you will meet

Intake — a scheduled telephone interview with an investigator. If the facts suggest discrimination may have occurred, the investigator drafts a formal complaint for you to sign.

Self-drafted complaint — you may write and file your own instead of going through intake. The Bureau publishes guidance on how.

Reasonable cause — the investigator’s finding that it is more likely than not that discrimination occurred. No reasonable cause is the opposite and results in dismissal.

Conciliation — if reasonable cause is found, Bureau staff attempt to settle the case before any hearing.

Dual filing — if your allegations also fall under federal employment discrimination law, the complaint can be filed with the EEOC at the same time.

Exhaustion — Montana has no private right of action for discrimination claims. You cannot file in district court first — you must go through the Bureau. This is why the 180 days matters so much.

The areas it covers

The Bureau investigates discrimination in employment, housing, education, public accommodations, credit, finance and insurance (sex and marital status only), and state and local governmental agencies or municipalities.

The protected grounds

The Bureau’s own list, in its words: age, race, color, national origin, religion, creed, disability, marital status, sex, political belief (in government), vaccination status, or retaliation.

The core list is in § 49-2-303. Political belief in government service is § 49-3-201. Vaccination status was added separately in 2021.

Two ways Montana is broader than federal law: any age can bring an age claim here, not only people over forty. And in the Bureau’s words, “anyone who hires one or more persons is subject to the Montana Human Rights Act” — there is no minimum headcount.

Also unlawful: denying a reasonable maternity leave, or refusing to reinstate an employee after one.

The kinds of claim

Disparate treatment — you were treated worse than a comparable person because of a protected characteristic. The person you compare yourself to is a comparator, and finding a good one is often the whole case.

Disparate impact — a rule or test that looks neutral falls harder on one group and cannot be shown to be job-related and necessary. Intent is irrelevant. You prove it with numbers.

Hostile work environment — harassment severe or pervasive enough to alter the conditions of employment. It must be objectively hostile, not only hostile to you — and you do not have to prove psychological injury.

Retaliation — being treated badly because you complained, testified or opposed discrimination. Often the strongest claim available, because the timeline proves it.

Constructive discharge — conditions became so intolerable that a reasonable person would resign. Not a standalone claim — it is how you establish that a resignation was effectively a termination, so the underlying discrimination still counts. Resigning does not end your claim. Say in writing at the time why you are leaving, if you can.

Sex discrimination specifically

“Sex” is broader than most people assume. The first four below are well established under Montana law. The last two are more complicated, and the difference matters.

Pregnancy and childbirth — including related medical conditions. Montana treats this as sex discrimination and has a separate Maternity Leave Act at §§ 49-2-310 and 49-2-311.

Sexual harassment — a form of sex discrimination, not a separate category. It splits two ways — see below.

Quid pro quo — a job benefit or detriment made conditional on submitting to sexual conduct. A single incident can be enough.

Hostile environment harassment — conduct severe or pervasive enough to alter working conditions. It does not have to be sexual in nature — hostility directed at you because you are a woman counts, even with nothing sexual in it.

Sex stereotyping — being penalized for not conforming to expectations about how a woman or a man should look, behave, or what work they should do. Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins established this in 1989.

Sexual orientation and gender identity — neither is in the text of the Act, and the Legislature has repeatedly declined to add them. But the Bureau’s own current guidance lists both within “sex” — its published self-drafted-complaint page reads “sex (including sexual harassment, pregnancy, sexual orientation and gender identity).” In 2020, Maloney v. Yellowstone County held gender identity protected under the Act as a form of sex discrimination, following Bostock v. Clayton County.

So tick “sex” and say plainly which ground you mean. Maloney is not a Montana Supreme Court ruling, so the position could be argued — but you are relying on the Bureau’s own published list, which is a strong place to stand. Bostock also covers both federally, which you reach by dual-filing with the EEOC, and Bozeman, Butte, Helena, Missoula and Whitefish have their own local ordinances.

Where to check if your ground is unusual

The grounds written into § 49-2-303 are the settled ones. Some others — sexual orientation and gender identity among them — are not in the statutory text but appear on the Bureau’s own published list of what it accepts.

The Bureau’s self-drafted-complaint page is the thing to look at. It states the protected classes in the Bureau’s own words, and it is more current than the statute on this point. Where the two differ, the Bureau’s page tells you what it will take.

And if you are still unsure, ask the investigator directly at intake. It costs one question.

Appearance

Appearance is not a protected class. Not in Montana, not federally. There is no law against being targeted for how you look, and it is worth knowing that plainly before you file.

But it is often reachable through sex discrimination, three ways.

Sex stereotyping — Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins. Ann Hopkins was denied partnership and told to walk, talk and dress more femininely and wear makeup. The Court held that penalizing a woman for not conforming to expectations about how a woman should look or behave is sex discrimination.

Harassment need not be sexual — if hostility is directed at you because you are a woman, it counts with nothing sexual in it. Appearance-based attacks on a woman, in a workplace where men do not face them, are evidence of exactly that.

Woman-on-woman harassment counts — Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services (1998), decided unanimously, held that same-sex harassment is actionable. Harassment by a woman is not excused because she is a woman.

The hard part, honestly

You have to show the treatment was because of sex — not because someone personally disliked you. This is where appearance-based claims most often fail.

Oncale gives the test: whether members of one sex are exposed to disadvantageous terms the other sex is not. So build the pattern. Does this only happen to women here? Is the content sex-linked — looks, dress, motherhood, who she is married to? Would a man in the same position have faced it?

Three women targeted on appearance and no men is a pattern. One incident is a feud.

A note on physical standards

A physical test applied to everyone can still be sex discrimination. If it screens out women at a disproportionate rate and the employer cannot show it measures something the job actually requires, that is disparate impact — and intent does not matter.

Ask: has it ever been validated? Is there a job-task analysis? Has anyone studied its impact by sex? If the answer is no, say so in your complaint.

What the Bureau does NOT handle

In the Bureau’s own words, it does not take “hiring and firing decisions which do not relate to discrimination based upon the factors listed above, such as termination without good cause under the Montana Wrongful Discharge Act.”

That is a different statute and a different route — and in Montana it is unusually strong.

The four-fifths rule

A selection rate for any group below 80 percent of the highest group’s rate is evidence of adverse impact under the EEOC Uniform Guidelines.

Example: 22 of 32 men appointed is 68.75%. 1 of 5 women is 20%. That ratio is 29% — against a threshold of 80.

The doctrines that save claims

Continuing violation — if the conduct is ongoing rather than a single act, the clock runs from the most recent act, and earlier acts outside the deadline come in as part of the pattern. This matters enormously for hostile environment claims, which are cumulative by nature.

Say the words continuing violation in your complaint if the conduct is ongoing. Do not assume it will be inferred.

Pretext — the stated reason is not the real reason. Under the McDonnell Douglas framework you make a basic showing, they give a reason, and you show it is false. Documents are how you show pretext.

Mixed motive — where a decision had both a lawful and an unlawful reason behind it. Montana’s rule is harsher than the federal one — read it before you rely on it.

Mixed motive — ARM 24.9.611

If you prove discrimination and the respondent proves it would have taken the same action anyway, that is a mixed motive case.

In that situation the Commission will order the respondent to stop the conduct and may impose conditions to prevent repeats — but it will not award you compensation for an adverse action that would have happened regardless.

You can win and still receive nothing. That is why documenting that the stated reason is false — pretext — matters more than documenting that discrimination was one factor among several.

Adverse action — anything that would deter a reasonable person: discipline, exclusion, schedule changes, a bad reference, removal of training.

Protected activity — filing, testifying, assisting, or opposing discrimination. Speaking publicly about discrimination you experienced is protected activity.

Institutional ratification — when the employer, through someone with authority, endorses conduct rather than merely failing to stop it. A supervisor who likes, shares or publicly agrees with a post attacking you has done something different from a supervisor who said nothing — and it is worth far more in evidence, because it ties the conduct to the institution. Look for approval, not only silence.

Imputed knowledge — what the employer is treated as knowing because someone in authority knew it. If you reported something to a supervisor, the organization knew, whether or not it was passed on.

The rebuttal

When they answer your complaint, you get to answer back. Most people waste it.

What happens, in order

1. You file. The Bureau notifies the respondent — usually within 10 days. By statute it cannot start investigating until they are on notice.

2. The respondent files a position statement: their account, and their defense.

3. The Bureau sends it to you and asks you for a rebuttal. In the Bureau’s own words, a rebuttal is where you tell them why you do not agree with the respondent’s defense.

4. Then the investigation proper — witness interviews, document requests, comparative information, and sometimes a fact-finding conference or site visit.

This is the most important document you will write

Your complaint is two pages that state what happened. The rebuttal is where the case is actually fought, because it is the first time you are answering a specific defense rather than describing an experience.

And it lands at the moment the investigator is forming a view. Whatever you do not answer stands unanswered.

Put it through Claude first

Before you read it for the fifth time at midnight, hand it over. A position statement is written by someone whose job is to make it sound reasonable, and it is hard to see past that when it is about you.

Give it their statement, your complaint, your chronology and your exhibit index, and ask it to go through line by line.

The prompt

Here is the respondent’s position statement in my Human Rights Bureau case, with my complaint, my chronology and my exhibit list. Go through their statement line by line and tell me, for each point: what they admit, what they deny, what they have reframed rather than denied, and anything that is inaccurate or contradicted by my documents — quoting both sides. Then list every allegation in my complaint that their statement does not address at all. Do not soften anything and do not invent anything; if a claim of mine has no document behind it, say so.

Then check every single thing it tells you. Open the document it cites. Read the line in their statement yourself. It is finding candidates for you, not conclusions.

Write the rebuttal in your own words. Use what it found, not how it phrased it. The investigator will be reading your voice elsewhere in the file and it needs to match.

Why this works

The hard part of a rebuttal is not the writing. It is holding their whole document in mind against your whole record at once — which is exactly the thing a person cannot do and a machine can.

It is also better than you are at the third pass: noticing what is not there.

How to read their position statement

Read it three times, with a different question each time — yourself, after you have the list above.

First pass — what do they admit? Anything they concede is now established and you never have to prove it. Mark those and leave them alone.

Second pass — what do they deny, and what does my evidence say? For every denial, find the document. This is the spine of your rebuttal.

Third pass — what did they not mention at all? This is the pass most people skip and it is the most important one. Go claim by claim through your own complaint and tick off each one they answered. Whatever is left is silence, and silence on a specific allegation is worth pointing out.

Watch for the shift

A common pattern: they do not deny the conduct, they reframe it. It was a performance issue. It was a misunderstanding. Everyone is treated that way. She was difficult.

Answer the reframe directly, with dates. If performance became a concern only after you complained, the chronology says so and you should say it in a sentence.

How to write it

Go point by point, in their order. Quote their sentence, then answer it. An investigator holding both documents can then follow you without hunting.

Attach the document, every time. “That is untrue” is worth nothing. “That is contradicted by the email of 9 June, attached as Exhibit 14” is worth everything.

Correct facts, not characterizations. Let their adjectives go. Spend your words on dates, documents and who was present.

Name anything they did not answer. “The position statement does not address the allegation at paragraph C.” One line each.

Give the investigator work to do. If their account depends on a witness, name the person who contradicts it. If it depends on a record, say which record and who holds it.

Keep it calm. You will want to be furious. A rebuttal that reads as angry invites them to argue you are difficult — which may be the defense they are already running.

A structure that works

1. One short paragraph: what you are responding to and the date of their statement.

2. What they admit — briefly, so it is on the record as agreed.

3. Point by point: their words, your answer, your exhibit.

4. What they did not address at all.

5. One short closing paragraph. No argument, no plea. Just what the documents show.

Practical

Ask how long you have the moment it arrives, and ask in writing. Then put the date in your correspondence log.

Ask for an extension if you need one — before the deadline, not after, and in writing.

Number your exhibits and attach an index. Make it easy to find what you are citing.

Keep a copy of everything you send, with the date you sent it.

Log their position statement and your rebuttal on the Correspondence page, both with dates. That record matters later if you ever have to show what was before the investigator and when.

Mediation, and what to expect — from the guide

The Bureau provides voluntary resolution services free of charge, and its own staff are trained to mediate. It is completely voluntary and cannot be imposed on either party.

The two names for the same thing

ARM 24.8.301 draws a distinction worth knowing.

Mediation agreement — a voluntary resolution reached before the Bureau begins its informal investigation.

Conciliation agreement — a voluntary resolution reached after the Bureau issues a reasonable cause finding. If cause is found, the Bureau is required to attempt conciliation.

If the parties want to discuss resolution, the Bureau may add up to 45 days to the investigation period. If mediation fails, that 45 days is added.

Two rules that protect you

The Bureau cannot be a party to an agreement containing a “no rehire” provision. If a respondent wants you to agree never to work there again, the Bureau cannot approve it.

The Bureau discourages global releases. In its own words, such releases “go beyond the scope of the complaint before the Human Rights Bureau.” A global release asks you to give up claims that have nothing to do with what you filed.

The Bureau cannot be party to an agreement that violates a party’s statutory rights, and may refuse to approve one that does not resolve all allegations or all affected people.

What to expect in the room

It is not a hearing — nobody decides who is right. Nobody rules on your evidence. The mediator is not a judge and will not tell you whether your case is good.

You may be separated — many mediations run with each side in a different room and the mediator moving between them.

They will go first on numbers, usually low — a first offer is a position, not an assessment of your case. Do not read it as a verdict.

You are not required to accept anything — you can leave. The complaint continues. That is the whole point of it being voluntary.

Everything must be in writing — an agreement must be written, signed by the parties, and approved by the Bureau.

The Bureau must be told all the terms — parties are required to inform the Bureau of every term of any agreement reached while the complaint is pending.

Find out who is actually paying

This is the thing I wish I had known earliest. When you are in mediation with a public body, you are usually not negotiating with the people in the room. You are negotiating with an insurer.

Cities, counties, school districts and special districts in Montana typically carry liability coverage through a risk pool or a commercial carrier. The carrier appoints and pays defense counsel. The carrier, not the agency, usually decides what gets offered.

Three questions to answer before you walk in

1. Who insures them? For a public body this is usually discoverable — coverage, the carrier or risk pool, and premiums appear in budgets, meeting packets and board minutes. A records request reaches it too.

2. Who has settlement authority, and are they in the room? Ask the mediator directly, before the session: does the person present have authority to settle, and up to what figure? If the answer is that they have to call someone, you are negotiating with an absent decision-maker and should know that going in.

3. Does the governing body have to approve it? Many public settlements require a vote by the council, commission or board — sometimes in an open meeting. That changes the timeline and sometimes the confidentiality.

Why it changes how you prepare

An insurer thinks about exposure, not embarrassment — appeals to fairness land differently than a clear picture of what a hearing would cost them. Documented facts, named witnesses and a quantified claim are the language that moves a carrier.

Coverage limits are real — if you know roughly what the policy covers, you know roughly the universe you are negotiating inside.

Non-monetary terms may sit outside the insurer’s remit — a carrier pays money. A policy change, training, or reinstatement is the agency’s to give, not the insurer’s. Those two things may need to be negotiated with different people in the same room — which is another reason to raise them early and separately.

Public money is public — what a public body paid to settle is generally a public record afterward. That cuts both ways, and it is worth knowing before you agree to a confidentiality term.

How I prepared

Decide your number before you walk in — and write down what each component is for. Being able to say “this figure is for X, this for Y” is far stronger than a single lump you cannot explain.

Decide what you will not trade — for me that was the policy and the test. Money was never the point. Know in advance which terms you would walk away over.

Non-disparagement. Usually broader than confidentiality. Often mutual — check that it is.

Anything about future employment. The Bureau cannot approve a no-rehire term.

If you are trying to change something

A settlement can include things money cannot. Policy changes. Training. A written statement. Validation of a test. Reporting requirements. Reinstatement.

Ask for them explicitly and in writing, and ask early. Non-monetary terms are far easier to get agreed before a number is on the table than after, because once the conversation is about money, everything else looks like an obstacle to closing.

And know that after a cause finding the Bureau itself may seek affirmative relief to correct or prevent discrimination — which means the public interest in the outcome does not rest only on you.

If you get “no reasonable cause”

A dismissal is not the end. But the clock is very short.

14 days. From the date on the letter.

You have 14 days to object to the Human Rights Commission — and the Bureau’s own flow chart is explicit about the trap: the 14 days runs from the date on the cover letter sent with the Final Investigative Report, not from the day you received it.

If it sat in your mailbox for four days, you have ten left. Open the envelope the day it arrives and write the deadline on the front of it.

The objection must be received by 5:00 p.m. at the office of the Human Rights Commission. Not postmarked. Received.

An objection filed late is dismissed as late. There is no discretion in it.

What happens next

Your opening brief is due within 21 calendar days of the decision to dismiss (ARM 24.9.121).

Their response is due 14 calendar days after yours.

Your reply is due 14 calendar days after theirs.

If you want to argue in person, you must ask for oral argument in the objection itself or in your first brief. Ask later and it is too late.

The Commission hears the appeal within 120 days and issues the final agency decision.

What you have to argue — and it is not what you expect

The Commission does not re-decide your case. It is not a second investigation and it is not a fresh look at the evidence.

The test is whether the Bureau abused its discretion

That is a high bar, and it is a different argument from “the investigator got it wrong.” You are not saying the conclusion was mistaken. You are saying the process was defective.

Things that go to that argument:

Witnesses you named were never interviewed. List them, with the dates you provided the names and what each would have said.

Evidence you submitted is not addressed anywhere in the report. Identify it by name and date.

A claim in your complaint was not investigated at all. Quote the claim from your complaint and show the silence in the report.

The report states a fact that your documents contradict. Put them side by side.

The report relied on an account that was never tested against documents you had provided.

Keep it to what is in the record. The Commission is reviewing what the Bureau did with what it had.

The other route

You can instead file a new proceeding in district court. That is a different path with different rules, different costs, and a different timetable, and it is the point at which most people should talk to an attorney even if they have done everything themselves so far.

Do not let the 14 days pass while you think about it. Objecting does not stop you taking advice. Missing the window does close a door.

Working with Claude

What I actually used it for, what it found, and the rules I never broke.

What it does that a person cannot

Not legal judgment. Scale.

It reads everything at once. You can search a folder for a word you remember. It can hold two thousand documents in mind at the same time and tell you how they relate. Those are different abilities.

It compares two documents line by line without getting tired. Thirty-four differences between two versions of the same minutes is the kind of thing a person stops seeing after the fifth page.

It finds a person across years. Every appearance, in date order, from every file — including the ones you forgot you had.

It notices absence. Humans read a letter to see what it says. Asking what is missing from it, against your own report, is a different question — and it is the one that matters.

It reads metadata, headers and properties that you would never open.

It does not get discouraged at eleven at night in the middle of a long year.

What it cannot do

It cannot decide your case, give you legal advice, or know anything you have not given it. It is confident when it is wrong and it cannot tell you which is which. Everything below is written on that basis.

What changed

By the middle of last summer I had thousands of files and one memory. I could search for a word if I remembered the word. Connections between documents months apart were invisible to me, because I could not hold them in my head at the same time.

I connected my evidence folder to Claude and started asking questions across the whole record at once. Not “summarize this document” — questions like where else does this person appear, and what contradicts this letter, and what is missing that should be here.

What that turned up

A set of official minutes quietly republished with thirty-four differences from the version I had.

A speech sent from a government email account that echoed, almost word for word, a public post made the next day.

Three profile updates inside a single window, by people who had no obvious reason to act together.

Metadata on an image someone posted publicly showing it had been saved from a private message before it was posted — so it had been circulating before anything was public.

Patterns, not documents. Every one of those facts was already in my folder. I could not see any of them.

It also did the ordinary work: reading long documents I was too tired to read properly, keeping track of which version of something was current, telling me plainly what a letter required of me and by when.

And it argued with me. More than once I was told a claim I wanted to make was weaker than I thought, or that a sentence I had written was something I could not actually prove. That is the part that made the record solid. Anything that only tells you what you want to hear is worse than useless in a case file.

The rules I never broke

These matter more than anything else on this page

Never let it invent a fact, a quote or a date. Every factual claim in anything you file must trace to a document you can produce. If you cannot point to the source, cut the sentence.

Never cite a case it gave you without reading the case. People have been sanctioned in real courts for filing citations to decisions that do not exist.

Never let it write in a voice that is not yours. Your account has to sound like you, because it has to be you under questioning.

Never treat it as an authority. It is a research assistant whose work you always check. It is confident when it is wrong, and it cannot tell you which is which.

If you let it organize your evidence, check every placement yourself. It will put things in sensible-looking folders, and sensible-looking is not the same as right. A document filed under the wrong date or the wrong person is worse than one left in a pile, because you will stop looking for it. Have it propose the structure. You do the filing.

Think before you upload anything containing other people’s private information — medical details, identification numbers, children’s information. You are responsible for what you put there.

And keep a complete copy of your evidence offline. Never let a cloud account be the only place your case exists.

Looking after yourself

This part is not optional. Carrying a case alone is a load, and it is easier to put down than you think.

Keep your journal on paper

Write it by hand, in a book, dated, on the day. Not here, and not in a notes app.

A handwritten entry made at the time is contemporaneous evidence. It carries weight precisely because you wrote it before you knew how any of this would turn out. A page in a bound book, in your handwriting, dated, is hard to argue with.

Typed entries are easier to dispute. A file can be edited, back-dated or re-saved, and nothing on its face shows when the words were written. Paper does not have that problem.

Keep it factual. What happened, when, who was there, what was said. Feelings belong in it too — but keep them in their own paragraph, separate from the facts.

Box breathing — 90 seconds

In through the nose for four. Hold for four. Out through the mouth for six. Hold for two. Four rounds.

Where this sits in your body — five stretches

Stress collects in the same few places: jaw, neck, shoulders, chest, hips. Thirty seconds each, breathing out as you go further in. None of this needs a mat or a floor.

Jaw and face. Open your mouth as wide as it goes, hold five, release. Three times. Then press your thumbs under your cheekbones and hold. Most people clench all night without knowing.

Neck. Ear towards shoulder, hand resting on the side of your head — no pulling. Thirty seconds each side. Then chin to chest.

Shoulders and upper back. Hands clasped in front, round your back and push forward. Then clasp behind you and lift. Thirty seconds each.

Chest. Forearm flat on a door frame, elbow at shoulder height, step through. Everything about a hard year pulls you forward; this opens it back up.

Hips. Sit, ankle on the opposite knee, lean forward with a flat back. Thirty seconds each side. The hips hold more than people expect.

And the thing that works best

Walk. Twenty minutes outside, no phone, no podcast. It does more for a bad day than anything else on this page, and it is the one most people skip.

Start documenting now

Screenshot everything, immediately

Posts get deleted. Comments get edited. A trustee in my case published a post admitting that my training stopped after I complained, then edited that sentence out four days after my cease-and-desist. I had screenshotted it the day it went up, so I have both versions — which means I can show not only what he admitted, but exactly what he went back and removed.

The edit is its own evidence. A person who quietly deletes one sentence four days after a legal notice has told you which sentence mattered.

Not everything can be saved that way. A woman who threatened me deleted a post carrying over 111 comments after she called the police on me, and those comments are gone. The difference between the two outcomes was timing.

Capture it the day you see it. Not the week after.

How to screenshot so it counts

Include the whole frame. Name, date, timestamp, reaction count, comment count. A cropped quote proves nothing.

Capture the thread, not just the comment. Who they were replying to matters.

Get the reaction list. Tap into who reacted. In my case, an Assistant Chief reacting with a heart to a public post attacking me is a very important exhibit — it establishes institutional ratification, because a supervisor endorsed the conduct rather than merely failing to stop it. It only exists because I opened the reaction list.

Screenshot your own phone’s date and time showing. It is on the status bar. Do not crop it off.

Capture the profile too. A screenshot of someone’s page showing their name and relationship to the respondent ties the comment to the case.

Metadata — the part nobody thinks about

Every photo and screenshot carries hidden data: the date and time it was created, and sometimes where, and what device made it.

This can prove things nothing else can. In my case the metadata on an image someone posted publicly recorded that it had been saved from Messenger before it was posted — meaning the image was circulated privately first. That one detail turned several unconnected posts into evidence of coordination.

Working with metadata

Look at it. On an iPhone, open the photo and swipe up, or tap the info button. On Android, open the photo and tap details. You will see the date, time, device, and sometimes the source app.

Screenshot the metadata panel itself. That screenshot is your exhibit.

Do not edit, crop, or re-save the original. Every time you do, you risk stripping it. Keep the untouched original and work from copies.

Beware of sharing methods that strip it. Texting a photo or uploading to some platforms removes metadata. Transfer originals by AirDrop, cable, or direct upload to cloud storage.

Your own photos carry it too. A photo I took of a note left in the station kitchen on my first training night carried a timestamp proving when it happened. I did not plan that. It was simply there.

Check their website. Then check it again.

Public bodies publish an enormous amount without realizing what is in it. Agendas, packets, minutes, audio and video recordings, budgets, policy manuals, personnel rosters, board appointments. All of it sitting there, free, needing no request at all.

I found some of my most important evidence on a city council website. A recording of a public meeting — days after I first spoke publicly — in which a firefighter’s spouse stood up during public comment and said something about me that was not true. That recording was posted by the City itself. Nobody had to hand it to me.

What to look for

Meeting recordings and minutes. Compare the two. Minutes are a summary written by a person. A recording is what was actually said. The gap between them is often the evidence.

Agendas and packets. These contain the documents given to decision-makers before a vote — letters, reports, recommendations. Often more revealing than the minutes.

Budgets and pay schedules. What a position is funded at, and who is actually being paid.

Policy manuals and handbooks. Note the adoption dates. A policy adopted shortly after you complained is itself a fact.

Board rosters and appointment records. Who sits on what, and since when.

Download it the day you find it

Websites change. Pages get edited. Recordings go missing. Documents get quietly replaced with different versions.

Save the file itself, not a link. Screenshot the page showing the file listed, with the date visible. Note the URL and the date you captured it.

Check the Wayback Machine at web.archive.org for how the page looked before. If something has been altered or removed, a prior capture may still exist — and the difference between the two is its own evidence.

Gaps matter too. If a body posts recordings for every meeting and then stops for a stretch, that absence is a documented fact you can ask about in writing.

And it is free and anonymous. Nobody is notified that you looked. You can do this tonight, before you have decided anything.

Contemporaneous records

A journal written the day it happened is worth more than any reconstruction. Date at the top. What happened, who was present, what was said, what you did about it. Factual. No adjectives you would not say under oath.

Text yourself. Email yourself. Tell one friend in writing. All of those are timestamped by a third party and are far harder to challenge than your memory.

In my case, texts I sent my partner in real time during an incident became evidence that the incident happened as I described it. I was not building a case. I was just telling someone.

Recording people — what Montana law actually says

Montana is usually called a “two-party consent” state. That is not quite what the statute says. Under § 45-8-213(1)(c) the offense is using a hidden device to record a conversation without the knowledge of all parties. It is better described as an all-party notification rule.

The practical answer: announce it

§ 45-8-213(2)(a)(iii) excepts anyone given warning of the recording — and adds that if one person gives the warning, either party may record.

So say it out loud at the start: “Before we go on, I am recording this call.” If they stay on the line, you are inside the exception. If they object, stop.

Say it on the recording itself, not in a message beforehand. The announcement and their response are then part of the file, which is exactly where you want them.

Public meetings — § 45-8-213(2)(a)(ii) excepts anyone speaking at a public meeting.

Officials in public — courts recognize a First Amendment right to record government officials carrying out their duties in public, covering audio as well as video, and Montana sits in the Ninth Circuit where that is established. The statute also reaches only a hidden device, and a public place carries no reasonable expectation of privacy. Hold the phone where it can be seen.

But not behind a closed door — a private meeting with your supervisor is a private conversation. The official-duty exception in (2)(a)(i) protects the official who records, not you. Announce it.

A recording made unlawfully is worse than no recording. Criminal exposure, probably excluded, and it hands the other side something to talk about instead of your evidence.

How to record a call

iPhone — Call Recording is built in on iOS 18.1 and later — tap the record button during a call. Everyone hears an automatic announcement, which conveniently satisfies the warning requirement. The recording and a transcript land in Notes.

Android — the Phone by Google app records on many devices and also announces it to both parties.

Either — speaker phone plus a second device. Announce it first.

Voicemail is different — a message someone left you is yours. Save it, back it up, transcribe it.

Turning audio into text

A recording nobody can search is barely evidence. Three hours of a council meeting proves nothing until you can point to the minute where someone says the thing.

On an iPhone, free — Voice Memos transcribes automatically — open a recording and tap the transcript button. It works on files you import, not only ones you record. Android has the same in the Recorder app.

For harder audio — Whisper is a free, open-source transcription model, noticeably better on poor recordings. MacWhisper, Whisper Desktop or Buzz let you drop in a file. It runs on your own machine, so nothing is uploaded.

Ask for timestamps — so you can cite “at 1:14:22” rather than “somewhere in the second hour.”

Never replace the recording with the transcript — the audio is the evidence; the transcript is the index to it. And check the transcript against the audio before quoting — every tool mishears names, and names are usually the part that matters.

Organizing it

A folder of three thousand screenshots is not evidence. It is a pile. The difference between the two is structure, and structure is what lets you find the one document that answers a question in the ten minutes you have before a call.

How I organize

There is no single right system. This is mine, and it survived two years and several thousand files.

By respondent — One folder per entity you are complaining about. Everything touching that entity goes in its folder, even if it also belongs somewhere else. Duplicate rather than agonize.

By person — For individuals who appear repeatedly. I have folders for specific people whose conduct spans years. When a new post appears, it goes straight in.

By event — For things that happened on one date and generated many documents — a meeting, an incident, a campaign.

By source — Records produced to you, separated by who produced them and when. Label with the date of production. This matters when they produce something late, or produce different versions.

Filings — What you have sent and what they have sent. Every version. Never overwrite.

Working — Drafts, notes, half-finished analysis. Keep it separate from evidence so you never confuse the two.

Naming files so you can find them

Date first, in YYYY-MM-DD. It sorts chronologically on its own.

Never rename an original you may need to prove is unaltered. Copy it, rename the copy.

Keep an index. One document listing every exhibit, its date, what it shows, and where it lives. Mine is the single most useful file I have.

Back it up in three places

Cloud storage, your own computer, and something physical you keep somewhere else. Evidence that exists in one place does not really exist.

Do not keep the only copy on a work device or a work account. Access can be cut off without warning, and often is.

Getting the records

Almost everything I was able to prove came from records someone else created and had to hand over. Not from my account of events. From their own documents, contradicting their own statements.

Who is subject to a request

In Montana the right to know is in the Constitution itself — Article II, Section 9 — not merely in statute. It reaches public bodies and agencies of state and local government.

That includes: cities and towns, counties, school districts, fire districts and other special districts, state agencies and departments, public universities, and the boards and committees of all of them.

It does not reach private employers. If your employer is private, this route is closed — but your own personnel file may still be available under a different rule, and discovery in litigation reaches further than a records request ever will.

What people miss

Text messages on personal devices about public business are public records. So are emails sent from a private account about government work. The test is the content, not the device.

Meeting recordings are records. So are draft minutes, calendars, call logs, and the metadata on documents.

Ask for the search terms they used. If a production comes back thin, asking what they searched and which accounts they searched is often more revealing than the production itself.

How to make the request

In writing, always — email is fine. Keep the sent copy. The date you asked is as important as what you asked for.

Be specific about scope — name the date range, the people whose records you want, and the accounts — official and personal — you believe hold responsive material.

Ask for categories, not conclusions — “all communications between X and Y between these dates” works. “Anything showing they discriminated” does not.

Ask about fees up front — Montana allows a charge for the actual cost of fulfilling a request. Ask for an estimate before they begin, and ask whether a waiver is available.

Follow up in writing when it is late — and keep following up. A paper trail of unanswered requests is itself evidence.

Ask again, differently — I obtained the two documents most central to my case only after a different body produced them from an account the first search had excluded.

If they are a public body, the meeting rules are yours too

Montana’s open meeting law is not just about watching. It gives you things you can use, and it is written to be liberally construed in favor of openness.

They cannot act on an item that is not on the published agenda — § 2-3-103. A decision taken on something the public was never told would be discussed is vulnerable — and a district court can void a decision made in violation of the open meeting law.

You have a right to comment on matters not on the agenda — the agenda must include an item allowing public comment on any public matter within the agency’s jurisdiction that is not otherwise listed. They do not get to keep your subject off the agenda and thereby keep you quiet.

What you say must go into the minutes — § 2-3-103 requires public comment received at a meeting to be incorporated into the official minutes. If you speak, there is a permanent record of it — and if the minutes omit or distort what you said, that omission is itself documented.

Minutes must be kept and available — for every meeting required to be open. Compare them against the recording.

When they can close a meeting — and when they cannot

Individual privacy. § 2-3-203(3). But the right belongs to the person being discussed — and if that person waives it, the meeting must be open. If they are closing a session to talk about you, you can waive and force it open.

Litigation strategy. § 2-3-203(4). Only where an open meeting would have a detrimental effect on their litigating position — and never where the only parties to the litigation are public agencies.

The possibility of a closed session must appear on the agenda so the public is notified in advance.

And the decision itself must be made in open session. They may discuss strategy behind a closed door. They may not decide behind one.

Minutes of a properly closed session are sealed absent a court order — but minutes of that closed portion must still be kept. The absence of them is a fact.

What becomes public afterward

A settlement paid by a public body is generally a public record. Public money is public. So are the minutes approving it, and in many cases the agreement itself.

This cuts both ways and you should know it before you sign anything. A confidentiality term cannot un-public a public expenditure, and a term that purports to may not be enforceable against the record itself. Ask explicitly what the body will disclose if someone requests it.

It also means you can look up what they paid to settle with other people. Prior settlements, verdicts, and the budget lines they came out of are often discoverable before you ever walk into mediation.

Your own records

Separately from any public records request, ask for your own file. Personnel file, training records, disciplinary records, your application, anything with your name on it.

Ask in writing. Ask for the complete file, including anything held by a supervisor outside the official file. Ask for it in native format with metadata intact, not printed and rescanned.

Why your own file matters

My personnel file arrived 39 days late and contained a written warning I had never seen or been told about. It was dated, signed, and filed without my knowledge.

It also showed what was not there — no record of the training that had been halted, no record of a commitment that had been made to me in writing elsewhere. Absences in a file are evidence too.

Ask early. If you wait until you are in a dispute, a litigation hold may be invoked to delay it.

The words

You cannot argue with a system whose language you do not speak. Learn these first.

The two sides

Charging party — what the Bureau calls the person filing the complaint. You will also see complainant. Same person.

Respondent — the business or entity the complaint is against. Montana’s Act reaches organizations, not individuals personally.

The three layers

There are three possible stages of review, and knowing which one you are in tells you what can happen next.

Human Rights Bureau — the neutral state agency that receives complaints and conducts the informal investigation. The Bureau does not award damages.

Office of Administrative Hearings — where a case goes after a reasonable cause finding. A hearing examiner holds a contested case hearing and can award damages. Older material calls this the Hearings Bureau.

Human Rights Commission — a five-member citizen panel appointed by the Governor. Hears appeals from either a Bureau dismissal or a hearing examiner’s decision.

Words you will meet

Intake — a scheduled telephone interview with an investigator. If the facts suggest discrimination may have occurred, the investigator drafts a formal complaint for you to sign.

Self-drafted complaint — you may write and file your own instead of going through intake. The Bureau publishes guidance on how.

Reasonable cause — the investigator’s finding that it is more likely than not that discrimination occurred. No reasonable cause is the opposite and results in dismissal.

Conciliation — if reasonable cause is found, Bureau staff attempt to settle the case before any hearing.

Dual filing — if your allegations also fall under federal employment discrimination law, the complaint can be filed with the EEOC at the same time.

Exhaustion — Montana has no private right of action for discrimination claims. You cannot file in district court first — you must go through the Bureau. This is why the 180 days matters so much.

The areas it covers

The Bureau investigates discrimination in employment, housing, education, public accommodations, credit, finance and insurance (sex and marital status only), and state and local governmental agencies or municipalities.

The protected grounds

The Bureau’s own list, in its words: age, race, color, national origin, religion, creed, disability, marital status, sex, political belief (in government), vaccination status, or retaliation.

The core list is in § 49-2-303. Political belief in government service is § 49-3-201. Vaccination status was added separately in 2021.

Two ways Montana is broader than federal law: any age can bring an age claim here, not only people over forty. And in the Bureau’s words, “anyone who hires one or more persons is subject to the Montana Human Rights Act” — there is no minimum headcount.

Also unlawful: denying a reasonable maternity leave, or refusing to reinstate an employee after one.

The kinds of claim

Disparate treatment — you were treated worse than a comparable person because of a protected characteristic. The person you compare yourself to is a comparator, and finding a good one is often the whole case.

Disparate impact — a rule or test that looks neutral falls harder on one group and cannot be shown to be job-related and necessary. Intent is irrelevant. You prove it with numbers.

Hostile work environment — harassment severe or pervasive enough to alter the conditions of employment. It must be objectively hostile, not only hostile to you — and you do not have to prove psychological injury.

Retaliation — being treated badly because you complained, testified or opposed discrimination. Often the strongest claim available, because the timeline proves it.

Constructive discharge — conditions became so intolerable that a reasonable person would resign. Not a standalone claim — it is how you establish that a resignation was effectively a termination, so the underlying discrimination still counts. Resigning does not end your claim. Say in writing at the time why you are leaving, if you can.

Sex discrimination specifically

“Sex” is broader than most people assume. The first four below are well established under Montana law. The last two are more complicated, and the difference matters.

Pregnancy and childbirth — including related medical conditions. Montana treats this as sex discrimination and has a separate Maternity Leave Act at §§ 49-2-310 and 49-2-311.

Sexual harassment — a form of sex discrimination, not a separate category. It splits two ways — see below.

Quid pro quo — a job benefit or detriment made conditional on submitting to sexual conduct. A single incident can be enough.

Hostile environment harassment — conduct severe or pervasive enough to alter working conditions. It does not have to be sexual in nature — hostility directed at you because you are a woman counts, even with nothing sexual in it.

Sex stereotyping — being penalized for not conforming to expectations about how a woman or a man should look, behave, or what work they should do. Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins established this in 1989.

Sexual orientation and gender identity — neither is in the text of the Act, and the Legislature has repeatedly declined to add them. But the Bureau’s own current guidance lists both within “sex” — its published self-drafted-complaint page reads “sex (including sexual harassment, pregnancy, sexual orientation and gender identity).” In 2020, Maloney v. Yellowstone County held gender identity protected under the Act as a form of sex discrimination, following Bostock v. Clayton County.

So tick “sex” and say plainly which ground you mean. Maloney is not a Montana Supreme Court ruling, so the position could be argued — but you are relying on the Bureau’s own published list, which is a strong place to stand. Bostock also covers both federally, which you reach by dual-filing with the EEOC, and Bozeman, Butte, Helena, Missoula and Whitefish have their own local ordinances.

Where to check if your ground is unusual

The grounds written into § 49-2-303 are the settled ones. Some others — sexual orientation and gender identity among them — are not in the statutory text but appear on the Bureau’s own published list of what it accepts.

The Bureau’s self-drafted-complaint page is the thing to look at. It states the protected classes in the Bureau’s own words, and it is more current than the statute on this point. Where the two differ, the Bureau’s page tells you what it will take.

And if you are still unsure, ask the investigator directly at intake. It costs one question.

Appearance

Appearance is not a protected class. Not in Montana, not federally. There is no law against being targeted for how you look, and it is worth knowing that plainly before you file.

But it is often reachable through sex discrimination, three ways.

Sex stereotyping — Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins. Ann Hopkins was denied partnership and told to walk, talk and dress more femininely and wear makeup. The Court held that penalizing a woman for not conforming to expectations about how a woman should look or behave is sex discrimination.

Harassment need not be sexual — if hostility is directed at you because you are a woman, it counts with nothing sexual in it. Appearance-based attacks on a woman, in a workplace where men do not face them, are evidence of exactly that.

Woman-on-woman harassment counts — Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services (1998), decided unanimously, held that same-sex harassment is actionable. Harassment by a woman is not excused because she is a woman.

The hard part, honestly

You have to show the treatment was because of sex — not because someone personally disliked you. This is where appearance-based claims most often fail.

Oncale gives the test: whether members of one sex are exposed to disadvantageous terms the other sex is not. So build the pattern. Does this only happen to women here? Is the content sex-linked — looks, dress, motherhood, who she is married to? Would a man in the same position have faced it?

Three women targeted on appearance and no men is a pattern. One incident is a feud.

A note on physical standards

A physical test applied to everyone can still be sex discrimination. If it screens out women at a disproportionate rate and the employer cannot show it measures something the job actually requires, that is disparate impact — and intent does not matter.

Ask: has it ever been validated? Is there a job-task analysis? Has anyone studied its impact by sex? If the answer is no, say so in your complaint.

What the Bureau does NOT handle

In the Bureau’s own words, it does not take “hiring and firing decisions which do not relate to discrimination based upon the factors listed above, such as termination without good cause under the Montana Wrongful Discharge Act.”

That is a different statute and a different route — and in Montana it is unusually strong.

The four-fifths rule

A selection rate for any group below 80 percent of the highest group’s rate is evidence of adverse impact under the EEOC Uniform Guidelines.

Example: 22 of 32 men appointed is 68.75%. 1 of 5 women is 20%. That ratio is 29% — against a threshold of 80.

The doctrines that save claims

Continuing violation — if the conduct is ongoing rather than a single act, the clock runs from the most recent act, and earlier acts outside the deadline come in as part of the pattern. This matters enormously for hostile environment claims, which are cumulative by nature.

Say the words continuing violation in your complaint if the conduct is ongoing. Do not assume it will be inferred.

Pretext — the stated reason is not the real reason. Under the McDonnell Douglas framework you make a basic showing, they give a reason, and you show it is false. Documents are how you show pretext.

Mixed motive — where a decision had both a lawful and an unlawful reason behind it. Montana’s rule is harsher than the federal one — read it before you rely on it.

Mixed motive — ARM 24.9.611

If you prove discrimination and the respondent proves it would have taken the same action anyway, that is a mixed motive case.

In that situation the Commission will order the respondent to stop the conduct and may impose conditions to prevent repeats — but it will not award you compensation for an adverse action that would have happened regardless.

You can win and still receive nothing. That is why documenting that the stated reason is false — pretext — matters more than documenting that discrimination was one factor among several.

Adverse action — anything that would deter a reasonable person: discipline, exclusion, schedule changes, a bad reference, removal of training.

Protected activity — filing, testifying, assisting, or opposing discrimination. Speaking publicly about discrimination you experienced is protected activity.

Institutional ratification — when the employer, through someone with authority, endorses conduct rather than merely failing to stop it. A supervisor who likes, shares or publicly agrees with a post attacking you has done something different from a supervisor who said nothing — and it is worth far more in evidence, because it ties the conduct to the institution. Look for approval, not only silence.

Imputed knowledge — what the employer is treated as knowing because someone in authority knew it. If you reported something to a supervisor, the organization knew, whether or not it was passed on.

The deadline
180 daysfrom the discriminatory act, or from when you discovered it · § 49-2-501

Roughly six months. It is the single most common reason a good claim is lost, and because Montana has no private right of action, missing it closes the courthouse too.

Three things to know

File first, perfect later — an imperfect complaint on day 170 beats a flawless one on day 190. The Bureau can amend. It cannot resurrect.

Or when you discovered it — the clock can run from discovery, not only from the act. If you learned about something later, say when and how you learned it.

Retaliation restarts the clock — every retaliatory act is its own violation with its own 180 days. If they retaliate six months after you file, that is a new claim, not a late one.

Two different 180-day periods

Yours: 180 days to file.

Theirs: by law the Bureau has 180 days from filing to complete its informal investigation.

They are easy to confuse. The one that can end your case is the first.

Filing

Whether the investigator drafts it or you do, the same things have to be in it.

The thing nobody warns you about

The complaint the investigator drafts is not the same as the account you gave. It is shorter, framed in statutory language, and limited to what the Bureau believes it can investigate. Things you consider central can disappear in that translation.

Go into intake with a written account already prepared. Send it in writing as well as saying it aloud. Then read the draft against your own account line by line — you have to sign it, so if something important is missing, say so before you do.

What a complaint needs

Who — the respondent’s correct legal name. Not the informal name of the department or office — the entity that employs or governs.

What — specific acts, not conclusions. Not “they created a hostile environment” but “on June 9, X did Y in front of Z.”

When — a date for every act. Approximate if you must, and say it is approximate.

Why it was discriminatory — the comparator. The pattern. The timing against your protected activity.

Which grounds — name them. Sex, retaliation, disability. Name more than one if more than one applies.

The most recent act, dated — for the deadline and for continuing violation.

What you want — reinstatement, back pay, policy change, training. You can revise later.

Drafting your own — the Bureau’s rules

You do not have to go through intake. The Bureau publishes guidance for filing a self-drafted complaint, and it is short. These are its requirements, not mine.

What a self-drafted complaint must contain

No more than 2 pages. That is the Bureau’s stated limit. Attach your chronology and documents separately.

1. Your full name, mailing address, email address and telephone number — and your birth year if this is an age discrimination complaint.

2. The full name, address and telephone number of the company or entity — and state whether it has over 15 employees.

3. A brief, clear and concise statement of the facts, with the relevant dates, and the protected class you say is the basis.

Sign and date it before sending. An unverified complaint will be dismissed — ARM 24.8.203 says so expressly.

File it by mail, personal delivery, or fax. Fax (406) 443-3234 · Human Rights Bureau, P.O. Box 1728, Helena, MT 59624-1728.

One more rule worth knowing. Under ARM 24.8.203, if your complaint does not state facts amounting to a claim the Act covers, the Bureau will tell you it will not investigate unless you amend it. That is not a rejection. It is an invitation to fix it — so if you get that letter, amend and refile rather than giving up.

The form itself

The Bureau normally drafts the charge from your intake interview and sends it to you to sign. You may also draft and file your own. Either way it helps enormously to have seen the form before you are looking at your own name on it.

A replica follows, annotated — what belongs in every box, and the two words you have to write yourself if they apply. A blank copy is available separately so you can print one and practice, or draft a real one.

Before you fill anything in

The purple text is guidance, not part of the form. The real form has none of it.

The Particulars box is where people over-write. This page states the case; it does not prove it. Five to eight lettered paragraphs is normal. Attach your chronology and exhibits rather than cramming them in.

You are signing under penalty of perjury. If you are unsure of a date, write “on or about”. If you do not know something, say you do not know it.

Three habits that matter more than anything else

Put everything in writing. A phone call not confirmed by email did not happen. After every conversation that matters: “Confirming our call today, you said X.” Keep it factual. That email is now evidence.

Keep a contemporaneous journal. Dated, same day, factual. A journal written as events occur carries weight a reconstruction never will.

Request records early and in writing. Public bodies in Montana are subject to the right to know under Article II, Section 9 of the Montana Constitution and the public records statutes beginning at § 2-6-1001. Ask for everything — emails, texts on personal devices used for public business, minutes, recordings, your own personnel file.

Name every claim, explicitly

This is where I got stuck, and where most people do.

The Bureau investigates what the complaint says, and only that. Not what the facts suggest, not what you clearly meant, not what is obvious from reading it. If a claim is not named, it is not in the case.

What that means in practice

If you were worn down over a year by comments, exclusion and hostility, you may think the complaint obviously describes a hostile work environment. It does not — unless you write those words.

If you resigned because conditions became intolerable, that is constructive discharge, and it is a different claim from the discrimination that caused it. Name it separately.

If something happened after you complained, that is retaliation, which is its own violation with its own 180 days. Name it again.

Three things happened to you. If you only name one, only one gets investigated.

Claims to consider naming

Disparate treatment — you were treated worse than someone comparable, because of a protected ground.

Hostile work environment — conduct severe or pervasive enough to alter the conditions of your work. Say the words.

Retaliation — something adverse happened because you complained, testified, or opposed discrimination.

Constructive discharge — you resigned because the conditions were intolerable.

Failure to accommodate — a disability or religious accommodation refused or ignored.

Disparate impact — a rule or test applied to everyone that screens out one group at a much higher rate, and has never been validated.

Denial of services or benefits — for claims against a government body.

Use the intake interview for exactly this

The intake worker does this every day and you do not. Describe everything that happened, in order, and let them tell you what it is called. That is the single most useful thing they do.

Then read the draft they write before you sign it, line by line, against your own account. If a claim you described is missing, say so before you sign. It is far easier to get it in now than to amend later.

How the process runs

Contact the Bureau
Call (406) 444-2884 or 1-800-542-0807. If you are only asking for information, the Bureau does not contact your employer.

Intake interview
If the matter is within the Bureau’s jurisdiction, a telephone interview is scheduled with an investigator.

The complaint is drafted
If the facts suggest discrimination may have occurred, the investigator drafts a formal complaint for you to sign. You may also draft and file your own.

Filed and served
Within 10 days the Bureau sends a copy of the complaint and your documents to the respondent. They have 10 business days to respond.

Assigned
Within 45 days of filing, the case is assigned to an investigator.

Investigation
Both parties may request copies of anything filed. By law the investigation must finish within 180 days of filing — 120 days for housing cases.

Finding
Reasonable cause — more likely than not that discrimination occurred. No reasonable cause — dismissal, and you may file in district court or appeal.

Conciliation
If reasonable cause is found, Bureau staff attempt to settle the case.

Contested case hearing
If conciliation fails, the case goes to the Office of Administrative Hearings. A hearing examiner issues a Final Agency Decision and can award damages.

Appeal
Within 14 days either party may appeal to the Human Rights Commission, which issues the final agency decision.

What the Bureau will and will not do

It is neutral. It does not advocate for you or for them, and it does not advise people whether to file.

It does not award damages. Only a hearing examiner can do that, after a contested case hearing.

It is free, and you do not need an attorney at any stage.

The rebuttal

When they answer your complaint, you get to answer back. Most people waste it.

What happens, in order

1 — You file. The Bureau notifies the respondent, usually within 10 days. By statute it cannot start investigating until they are on notice.

2 — The respondent files a position statement — their account, and their defense.

3 — The Bureau sends it to you and asks you for a rebuttal. In the Bureau’s own words, a rebuttal is where you tell them why you do not agree with the respondent’s defense.

4 — Then the investigation proper: witness interviews, document requests, comparative information, and sometimes a fact-finding conference or a site visit.

This is the most important document you will write

Your complaint is two pages that state what happened. The rebuttal is where the case is actually fought, because it is the first time you are answering a specific defense rather than describing an experience.

And it lands at the moment the investigator is forming a view. Whatever you do not answer stands unanswered.

Read their statement three times

First — what do they admit? — anything they concede is established and you never have to prove it. Mark those and leave them alone.

Second — what do they deny? — for every denial, find the document. This is the spine of your rebuttal.

Third — what did they not mention at all? — the pass most people skip, and the most important one. Go claim by claim through your own complaint and tick off each one they answered. Whatever is left is silence, and silence on a specific allegation is worth pointing out.

Watch for the shift

A common pattern: they do not deny the conduct, they reframe it. It was a performance issue. It was a misunderstanding. Everyone is treated that way. She was difficult.

Answer the reframe directly, with dates. If performance became a concern only after you complained, the chronology says so and you should say so in a sentence.

How to write it

Go point by point, in their order — quote their sentence, then answer it. An investigator holding both documents can follow you without hunting.

Attach the document, every time — “That is untrue” is worth nothing. “That is contradicted by the email of 9 June, attached as Exhibit 14” is worth everything.

Correct facts, not characterizations — let their adjectives go. Spend your words on dates, documents and who was present.

Name anything they did not answer — “The position statement does not address the allegation at paragraph C.” One line each.

Give the investigator work to do — if their account depends on a witness, name the person who contradicts it. If it depends on a record, say which record and who holds it.

Keep it calm — you will want to be furious. A rebuttal that reads as angry invites them to argue you are difficult — which may be the defense they are already running.

A structure that works

1. One short paragraph: what you are responding to, and the date of their statement.

2. What they admit — briefly, so it is on the record as agreed.

3. Point by point: their words, your answer, your exhibit.

4. What they did not address at all.

5. One short closing paragraph. No argument, no plea. Just what the documents show.

Ask how long you have the moment it arrives, and ask in writing. If you need an extension, ask before the deadline rather than after. Number your exhibits and attach an index.

Mediation, and what to expect

The Bureau provides voluntary resolution services free of charge, and its own staff are trained to mediate. It is completely voluntary and cannot be imposed on either party.

The two names for the same thing

ARM 24.8.301 draws a distinction worth knowing.

Mediation agreement — a voluntary resolution reached before the Bureau begins its informal investigation.

Conciliation agreement — a voluntary resolution reached after the Bureau issues a reasonable cause finding. If cause is found, the Bureau is required to attempt conciliation.

If the parties want to discuss resolution, the Bureau may add up to 45 days to the investigation period. If mediation fails, that 45 days is added.

Two rules that protect you

The Bureau cannot be a party to an agreement containing a “no rehire” provision. If a respondent wants you to agree never to work there again, the Bureau cannot approve it.

The Bureau discourages global releases. In its own words, such releases “go beyond the scope of the complaint before the Human Rights Bureau.” A global release asks you to give up claims that have nothing to do with what you filed.

The Bureau cannot be party to an agreement that violates a party’s statutory rights, and may refuse to approve one that does not resolve all allegations or all affected people.

What to expect in the room

It is not a hearing — nobody decides who is right. Nobody rules on your evidence. The mediator is not a judge and will not tell you whether your case is good.

You may be separated — many mediations run with each side in a different room and the mediator moving between them.

They will go first on numbers, usually low — a first offer is a position, not an assessment of your case. Do not read it as a verdict.

You are not required to accept anything — you can leave. The complaint continues. That is the whole point of it being voluntary.

Everything must be in writing — an agreement must be written, signed by the parties, and approved by the Bureau.

The Bureau must be told all the terms — parties are required to inform the Bureau of every term of any agreement reached while the complaint is pending.

Find out who is actually paying

This is the thing I wish I had known earliest. When you are in mediation with a public body, you are usually not negotiating with the people in the room. You are negotiating with an insurer.

Cities, counties, school districts and special districts in Montana typically carry liability coverage through a risk pool or a commercial carrier. The carrier appoints and pays defense counsel. The carrier, not the agency, usually decides what gets offered.

Three questions to answer before you walk in

1. Who insures them? For a public body this is usually discoverable — coverage, the carrier or risk pool, and premiums appear in budgets, meeting packets and board minutes. A records request reaches it too.

2. Who has settlement authority, and are they in the room? Ask the mediator directly, before the session: does the person present have authority to settle, and up to what figure? If the answer is that they have to call someone, you are negotiating with an absent decision-maker and should know that going in.

3. Does the governing body have to approve it? Many public settlements require a vote by the council, commission or board — sometimes in an open meeting. That changes the timeline and sometimes the confidentiality.

Why it changes how you prepare

An insurer thinks about exposure, not embarrassment — appeals to fairness land differently than a clear picture of what a hearing would cost them. Documented facts, named witnesses and a quantified claim are the language that moves a carrier.

Coverage limits are real — if you know roughly what the policy covers, you know roughly the universe you are negotiating inside.

Non-monetary terms may sit outside the insurer’s remit — a carrier pays money. A policy change, training, or reinstatement is the agency’s to give, not the insurer’s. Those two things may need to be negotiated with different people in the same room — which is another reason to raise them early and separately.

Public money is public — what a public body paid to settle is generally a public record afterward. That cuts both ways, and it is worth knowing before you agree to a confidentiality term.

How I prepared

Decide your number before you walk in — and write down what each component is for. Being able to say “this figure is for X, this for Y” is far stronger than a single lump you cannot explain.

Decide what you will not trade — for me that was the policy and the test. Money was never the point. Know in advance which terms you would walk away over.

Price the non-monetary terms — a global release, a confidentiality clause, a no-rehire provision, a non-disparagement term — each of these is something of yours that they are asking for. Put a number on each.

Bring the record — not to argue it, but so that when something is disputed you can produce the document in ten seconds.

Have your walk-away written down — in a state you will not be in after three hours in a room. Write it the night before.

Read every term before you sign

Confidentiality. What exactly can you not say afterward? Does it cover the underlying facts, or only the terms? Can you still speak about your own experience?

Non-disparagement. Usually broader than confidentiality. Often mutual — check that it is.

Release scope. Does it cover only this complaint, or everything that ever happened? The Bureau discourages the latter.

What happens to pending matters. If you have other complaints or intakes, say explicitly whether they are included.

Anything about future employment. The Bureau cannot approve a no-rehire term.

Tax treatment and timing of payment. And what happens if they do not pay.

If you are trying to change something

A settlement can include things money cannot. Policy changes. Training. A written statement. Validation of a test. Reporting requirements. Reinstatement.

Ask for them explicitly and in writing, and ask early. Non-monetary terms are far easier to get agreed before a number is on the table than after, because once the conversation is about money, everything else looks like an obstacle to closing.

And know that after a cause finding the Bureau itself may seek affirmative relief to correct or prevent discrimination — which means the public interest in the outcome does not rest only on you.

If you get “no reasonable cause”

A dismissal is not the end. But the clock is very short, and it is shorter than you think.

14 days — from the date on the letter

You have 14 days to object to the Human Rights Commission. The Bureau’s own flow chart is explicit about the trap: the 14 days runs from the date on the cover letter sent with the Final Investigative Report, not from the day you received it.

If it sat in your mailbox for four days, you have ten left. Open the envelope the day it arrives and write the deadline on the front of it.

The objection must be received by 5:00 p.m. at the office of the Human Rights Commission. Not postmarked. Received. An objection filed late is dismissed as late.

What happens next

Your opening brief — due within 21 calendar days of the decision to dismiss — ARM 24.9.121.

Their response — 14 calendar days after yours.

Your reply — 14 calendar days after theirs.

Oral argument — if you want to argue in person you must ask in the objection itself or in your first brief. Ask later and it is too late.

The hearing — the Commission hears the appeal within 120 days and issues the final agency decision.

What you actually have to argue

The Commission does not re-decide your case. It is not a second investigation and not a fresh look at the evidence.

The test is whether the Bureau abused its discretion

That is a high bar, and a different argument from “the investigator got it wrong.” You are not saying the conclusion was mistaken. You are saying the process was defective.

Things that go to that argument:

Witnesses you named were never interviewed — list them, with the dates you gave the names and what each would have said.

Evidence you submitted is not addressed anywhere — identify it by name and date.

A claim in your complaint was not investigated at all — quote the claim and show the silence in the report.

The report states a fact your documents contradict — put them side by side.

The report relied on an account never tested — against documents you had already provided.

Keep it to what is in the record. The Commission is reviewing what the Bureau did with what it had.

The other route

You can instead file a new proceeding in district court. Different rules, different costs, different timetable — and the point at which most people should talk to an attorney even if they have done everything themselves so far.

Do not let the 14 days pass while you think about it. Objecting does not stop you taking advice. Missing the window does close a door.

And read the report properly first

Go through the Final Investigative Report line by line against your own chronology and exhibit index. Mark every place where something you submitted is missing, mis-stated, or unaddressed.

That list is your objection. If it is long, you have an argument. If it is genuinely short, that is worth knowing too.

A separate Montana statute

Montana is the only state in the country where employment is not at-will — but that protection begins only once you have completed the employer’s probationary period.

So there are two phases, and which one you were in decides everything. During probation, either side can end the employment for any reason or none, exactly as in every other state. After probation, a discharge must be for good cause.

If you were discharged, the Wrongful Discharge from Employment Act may apply. But read the next box before you assume it does.

The two routes do not stack

§ 39-2-912 exempts the WDEA from applying to a discharge “subject to any other state or federal statute that provides a procedure or remedy for contesting the dispute” — and it names discrimination statutes specifically.

So if your discharge was discriminatory, that is a Human Rights Bureau matter, not a WDEA case. You generally do not get both for the same discharge. Which route applies depends on why you say you were fired.

The WDEA also does not apply to an employee covered by a written collective bargaining agreement or a written contract for a specific term.

Under § 39-2-904, a discharge is wrongful only if one of four things is true.

Public policy — it was in retaliation for your refusal to violate public policy, or for reporting a violation of public policy.

No good cause — the discharge was not for good cause and you had completed the employer’s probationary period.

Their own written policy — the employer materially violated an express provision of its own written personnel policy before the discharge, and that deprived you of a fair opportunity to keep your job.

Free speech — added in 2023 — the employer terminated you solely based on your legal expression of free speech, including but not limited to statements made on social media.

Find out exactly when your probation ended

This single date decides whether the good-cause protection applied to you. Get it in writing.

An employer that extends your probation extends the window in which it can fire you without cause. If yours was extended, ask why, ask for it in writing, and keep the answer — an extension that follows a complaint is itself worth examining.

The default where an employer sets no period has changed over the years — older versions of the statute presumed six months, and more recent sources describe twelve. Do not rely on a default. Ask for yours in writing and check the current text of § 39-2-904 yourself.

Note what the first and fourth grounds do not require. Retaliation for refusing to violate public policy, and termination for lawful speech, are wrongful whether or not you had completed probation. The probationary limit attaches to the good-cause ground.

Constructive discharge counts

Under § 39-2-903, “discharge” includes a constructive discharge — a resignation forced by intolerable conditions. Resigning does not automatically put you outside this statute.

How a WDEA claim actually works

No agency handles it — the Montana Department of Labor and Industry states plainly that the Wrongful Discharge from Employment Act is not administered by any state agency. There is no bureau to call. It is a lawsuit in district court.

Exhaust the internal grievance first — if your employer has an internal grievance procedure, you must use it before filing. If the employer does not complete it within 90 days, it may be treated as exhausted.

Check the filing deadline — the WDEA has its own limitation period, and it is not the Bureau’s 180 days. Look it up at Title 39, Chapter 2, Part 9 before you rely on any date.

Speaking publicly, and doing it carefully

Speaking publicly about discrimination you have experienced is protected activity. It is also one of the few things you can do that the other side cannot control. Used carelessly it costs you. Used deliberately it is the most powerful tool a self-represented person has.

Why it works

People respond. That is the whole mechanism. When I finally spoke publicly about my complaint, the response produced more admissible evidence in three weeks than eighteen months of asking politely had produced.

The most valuable single admission in my entire file came from a sitting Vice Chair of a fire district — an officeholder — who published a post stating that after my complaint “everything was put on hold and training stopped.” That is the retaliation, admitted, by someone with authority, in public, voluntarily.

He edited that sentence out four days after receiving my cease-and-desist. I had already screenshotted it.

What that episode teaches

Silence is not neutral. While you say nothing, the only account in circulation is theirs.

People defending an institution explain themselves. And in explaining, they confirm facts, admit sequences, and reveal who knew what. They are not being careful because they do not think they are creating evidence.

Capture it the moment it appears, and capture it again later. The admission that matters will be edited or deleted. Having the before and the after is worth more than having either one.

How much to say

Say enough to inform. Never say everything.

I put out just enough for my community to understand what had happened and to prompt a response.

I shared only documents that would make an impact and that revealed nothing capable of harming my case. Every single one was chosen. Nothing went out because I was angry, and nothing went out that I had not already thought through from the other side.

The dates, the contradictions, the analysis, and the documents whose value depended on the other side not knowing I had them — those stayed in the file.

Why holding back matters

What you have not disclosed still has value. What you have disclosed does not.

Organizations settle in part to buy confidentiality. That is normal and lawful — a confidentiality term is one of the things being exchanged in most settlements. But you cannot trade something you have already given away for free.

If every document is already public, the respondent has no reason to pay for your discretion. You will have spent your strongest card for nothing.

To be clear: this is about what you choose to disclose and when. It is never about threatening disclosure to extract money. That is a different thing entirely and it is unlawful.

The rules I follow

Only what I can prove — if I could not produce the document behind it tomorrow, I do not say it.

Facts, not conclusions — “My training stopped seven days after I filed” rather than “they retaliated against me.” The first is a date. The second is a legal conclusion I am asking someone else to reach.

Describe conduct, not character — what a person did, not what kind of person they are. This is also the line that keeps you clear of defamation.

Never the analysis — the pattern you have found is the thing you are holding. Showing it teaches the other side what to prepare for.

Expect it to be used against you — my own public speech was cited by the City as the reason I could not be reinstated. Assume every word will be read back to you, and write accordingly.

Before you post, check

Does my employer have a social media or speech policy? Read it. Policies adopted after you complain are themselves evidence — but you still have to live under them.

Am I stating a fact I can document, or an opinion about someone’s character?

Would I be comfortable reading this aloud at a hearing?

Am I giving away something I may want to trade later?

Using AI as a self-represented litigant

I want to be careful here, because this is the part most likely to be misused. But it is also the single thing that changed my case, and leaving it out would be dishonest.

What it is genuinely good for

Finding contradictions across documents — this is the real power. A person cannot hold four hundred documents in their head. I found a set of minutes that differed from a previously published version in thirty-four places. I would never have found that by reading.

Building timelines — feeding in documents and asking what happened on a given date, then checking it yourself against the source.

Explaining legal concepts — what a doctrine means, what a case held, what a statute requires. Then go read the statute.

Drafting and structure — turning a chronology into a readable account. The facts have to be yours. The organization can be helped.

Transcribing and reading records — long PDFs, meeting minutes, handwritten notes.

What it must never be used for

Never let it invent a fact, a quote or a date. Every factual claim in anything you file must trace to a document you can produce. If you cannot point to the source, cut the sentence.

Never cite a case it gave you without reading the case. People have been sanctioned for filing citations that do not exist. Open the case. Read the holding. Confirm it says what you were told.

Never let it write in a voice that is not yours. Your account has to sound like you, because it has to be you under questioning.

What actually broke my case open

Connecting my evidence directly to the AI. That was the turning point, and I want to be specific about why.

I use Google Drive and Claude. Claude can be connected to Drive, which means it can read the documents in my folders directly — I do not have to upload them one at a time or describe them from memory.

Why that changed everything

Before: I had thousands of files and a memory. I could search for a word if I remembered the word. Connections between documents months apart were invisible to me because I could not hold them in my head at once.

After: I could ask a question across the whole record. “Where else does this person appear?” “What was said about this on other dates?” “Does anything contradict this letter?”

What it found: a set of minutes quietly republished with thirty-four differences. A speech sent from a government email account that echoed a public post made the next day. Three profile updates in one window. A nine-year-old attack page recirculated during a campaign. Patterns, not documents.

How to set it up

Put your evidence in cloud storage — I use Google Drive. Organized in the folder structure described above — the organization is what makes it usable.

Connect it — in Claude, connectors are enabled in settings. Once connected it can read, search and reference your files directly.

Ask questions across the whole record — not “summarize this document” but “where else does this appear” and “what contradicts this.”

Verify every single thing — open the source document. Check the quote. Check the date. Treat it as a research assistant whose work you always check, never as an authority.

A caution about what you upload

Think before you connect anything containing other people’s private information — medical details, identification numbers, children’s information. You are responsible for what you put there.

And keep a complete copy offline. Do not let a cloud account be the only place your case exists.

Other tools worth knowing

Your phone — is a scanner. Most phones will produce a clean PDF from a photographed document.

Free PDF tools — for merging, splitting and searching across documents. Being able to search a 200-page production for a name is worth an hour of setup.

A spreadsheet — for the exhibit index. One row per exhibit: date, what it is, what it proves, where it lives.

The Wayback Machine — at web.archive.org. Captures of public pages as they existed on a date. If something has been edited or deleted, a prior capture may still exist.

Finding an attorney

Doing this yourself does not mean doing it alone. Those are different things, and I want to be clear about the difference.

What I actually did

I picked up the phone and started calling every employment lawyer I could find. I had help within an hour.

I did not retain anyone. I needed informal guidance through the investigation — someone to answer a question when I was unsure. If this reaches court, he can step in and we can sign a contingency agreement then.

I needed to do the work myself to be confident in it. I am the knowledge keeper of my own story. Nobody else was going to hold two years of detail the way I hold it, and I was not willing to hand that over and hope.

Every case is different

Some people have an attorney from the first day and it is the right call.

Some attorneys will not get involved until an investigation is complete, because until then there is nothing for them to assess.

Some will advise informally and step in later if it goes to court.

The only way to find out which you are dealing with is to pick up the phone.

How to make the call

Call several — not one. The first person you reach may be the wrong fit, too busy, or outside the area of law. That is not a verdict on your case.

Stay factual — dates, what happened, who was present, what document exists. Lead with the chronology, not the feeling.

Do not stuff the emotion either — you are allowed to be affected by what happened to you. A person who is clearly upset and still precise about dates is far more credible than someone performing calm. Just do not let it replace the facts.

Name the claims you think you have — “I think this is sex discrimination and retaliation” tells them in one sentence what area you are in. You do not have to be right. You have to be oriented.

Say what stage you are at — have you filed? Is there a deadline running? Has an investigation started? This decides whether they can help you at all right now.

Ask what they would need from you — and ask directly whether they take cases at your stage. A clear no saves you both time.

The thing most people do not know

Attorneys refer. A lawyer who cannot take your case very often knows someone who can, and will give you a name.

Several of the most useful conversations I had came from someone saying “I cannot help with this, but call so-and-so.” A referral from a lawyer carries more weight than a cold call ever will.

So always ask. “Is there someone else you would suggest I speak with?” costs nothing and is the single most productive question in the call.

What to have in front of you

Your chronology. The most recent act and its date. Your deadline. The name of the respondent. One or two documents you could send immediately if asked. Ten minutes of preparation changes the quality of the call entirely.

If cost is the obstacle

Many employment cases are contingency — meaning no fee unless there is a recovery. Ask directly — do not assume you cannot afford to ask.

Consultations are often free — and a consultation is not a commitment on either side.

Montana Legal Services Association — and the State Bar’s lawyer referral service are places to start if the private route is not working.

You do not need one to file — the Bureau process is free and designed to work without counsel. An attorney is an advantage, not a requirement.

The point is not to find someone to take it over. It is to stop guessing alone. There are questions you cannot answer by reading — whether a particular fact matters, whether a deadline applies to you, whether the thing worrying you is a real problem or not. Someone who does this for a living can tell you.

A last word

The right to represent yourself exists precisely so that justice does not depend on being able to afford it.

Most people walk away from a fight like this, and they walk away for a reason: they can see how much it will take and they know they do not have it. I almost walked away too. I did walk away, once.

The people who do this are counting on that. They know we do not have counsel on retainer.

Learn the words. Write everything down.
Request the records. File inside 180 days.

When you do, the person responsible is held to account — and the next one is spared.

What it does to you, and what to do about it

This is the part I would most want someone to read, and the part I understood last.

It isolates. That is not accidental.

The hardest thing about this is not the paperwork. It is that the longer it goes on, the smaller your world gets.

People take sides, and most of them take the institution’s. Not because they are cruel — because the institution is the thing they already belong to, and you are the one disturbing it. Friends get uncomfortable. Invitations stop. People you have known for years start choosing not to see you in the shop.

And you start doing it to yourself. You stop going places because you cannot face the conversation. You stop saying what you are doing because you are tired of the look. At some point you realize you have been alone with this for months and you cannot say exactly when that started.

What helped me

Tell a small number of people everything. Not everyone — a few. People who will hear the whole thing and not need you to perform being fine about it.

Keep one thing that has nothing to do with the case. Something you were doing before and will be doing after. Protect it. It is proof you are a person and not a complaint.

Set it down on purpose. Decide there are hours you do not touch it. The file will still be there. It is remarkably good at waiting.

Get professional support if you can. Not because something is wrong with you. Because carrying this alone is a load nobody should carry alone, and having one hour a week where you are not explaining yourself is worth a lot.

Learn to recognize DARVO

This has a name and knowing it is protective. DARVO stands for Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender. It is the pattern an institution or a person falls into when confronted.

Deny — it did not happen. Or it did not happen like that. Or nobody remembers it that way.

Attack — your credibility, your motives, your character. You are difficult. You are after money. You are enjoying the attention. You have done this before.

Reverse victim and offender — and this is the one to watch. Suddenly they are the ones being harmed. The department is “under attack.” The people who hurt you are the ones who need protecting from you.

Why naming it matters

When it works, DARVO does not just change other people’s minds. It changes yours.

You start wondering whether you did make too much of it. Whether you are difficult. Whether the kindest thing would be to stop. That is not insight arriving. That is the pattern working.

Once you can name what is happening, it loses a great deal of its power. You can look at a post calling you self-absorbed and think “that is the attack step” instead of “maybe I am.”

Do not let anyone turn your mistreatment into a verdict on you. What was done to you is a fact about them. Your response to it — even the angry parts, even the parts you would word differently now — is a human being responding to being harmed. Those are not the same thing and nobody gets to merge them.

Emotional harm is real harm

I want to be direct about this because I spent a long time not being.

There is a habit among people in our position of apologizing for the part that hurt most. We will talk readily about lost wages and lost training and documents withheld, and then go quiet about the fact that we stopped sleeping, or stopped going into town, or started checking the mirrors.

That is not a soft harm. It is the harm.

The law knows it too. Emotional distress is a recognized category of damage, not a courtesy. Hostile environment claims exist precisely because conduct that causes no financial loss at all can still be unlawful — and under Harris v. Forklift Systems you do not have to prove psychological injury for the conduct to be actionable. The law does not require you to have broken in order to have been wronged.

Ask for what you are owed

Do not be embarrassed about money. I was, for a while, and it cost me nothing but time.

A monetary settlement is not greed and it is not a substitute for accountability. In most cases it is the accountability — it is the only language in which an institution admits that what it did had a cost. An apology costs nothing. A payment comes out of a budget somebody has to answer for.

How to think about the number

Itemize it. Lost wages and benefits. Training you paid for or lost. Costs you incurred — records fees, postage, mileage, time off work. Future loss, if there is any. And emotional distress as its own line.

Being able to say what each figure is for is far stronger than a lump sum you cannot explain. Not “I want X” but “this figure is for that, and this figure is for this.”

Price the things they are asking you to give up. Confidentiality, a release, non-disparagement, agreeing not to reapply — each of those is something of yours. If they want it, it has a number.

And know what you would not trade at any price. For me that was the policy and the test. Decide that before you are in the room, not during.

What this makes you

I will not tell you this is good for you. It is not. I would give back a great deal of what I have learned in exchange for not having had to learn it.

But something does happen on the other side of it, and it is worth knowing it is coming.

You stop being frightened of being disliked. Once a town has decided about you and you are still standing, the fear of disapproval loses most of its grip. That is a strange kind of freedom and it does not go away afterward.

You learn you can do difficult things. Not because you felt capable — because you did them anyway, badly at first, while frightened. That knowledge transfers to everything else in your life.

And you find out who your people are. The list is shorter than you thought and better than you thought.

The person who comes out of this is more resilient than the one who went in. Not because the experience was a gift. Because you carried something heavy for a long way and your arms are stronger.

And it can change something

A finding closes a gap. A policy gets rewritten. A test gets validated. A procedure that failed you gets fixed before it reaches the next person.

I know that is possible because I have watched it happen once already, in a different part of my life. I went to law about something done to me as a child, and then I went to the legislature and had the limitation that nearly stopped me removed for everyone behind me. The second thing only existed because of the first.

That is what this is for. Not only the record being corrected — though it should be. Not only being paid what you are owed — though you should be.

The next woman who walks in should find an institution that has already been made to answer for this one.

Tara Walker

The one thing I would say

Start before you are sure you have a case.

Everything I was able to prove, I was able to prove because I had written it down at the time, screenshotted it before it disappeared, or asked for a record before anyone thought to withhold it.

The evidence that wins is almost never gathered after you decide to fight. It is gathered while you are still hoping you will not have to.

Tara Walker

A checklist

Everything here is something you can do today, without a lawyer and without permission.

Work out your deadline
Find the most recent discriminatory act, or the day you discovered it. Count 180 days forward. Write that date somewhere you will see it.

Write the chronology
Every date, what happened, who was present, what document exists. One line each.

Name the respondent correctly
The legal entity — not the informal name of the department or office.

Identify your comparator
Who was treated better in a comparable situation, and what shows the difference.

Gather what you already hold
Emails, texts, photographs, minutes, handbooks, your own notes.

Send one records request
In writing. Dated. Ask for more than you think you need.

Start the journal
Today’s date at the top. Keep it factual and keep it going.

Write your account before you call
Then send it in writing as well as saying it aloud.

Read the draft before you sign it
Line by line against your own account. Say so before signing if anything is missing.
